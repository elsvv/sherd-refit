# Task E1 — where the time and the memory go

**Date:** 2026-09-07. **Phase 1e, step E1.** Branch `rust-core`, from `87d75eb` ("Y8: the note
keeps V4's reduction of the release-only failure"); working tree clean at the start, nothing left
over from an earlier attempt. **Machine:** Apple M2 Pro, 10 cores (6 performance + 4 efficiency),
16 GB, macOS 24.6.0, rustc 1.97.0, `--release` (`lto = "thin"`, `codegen-units = 1`,
`debug = "line-tables-only"`). **Binary:** `target/release/sherd-refit-rs`. **Profiler:**
`samply` 0.13.1, sampled at 999 Hz.

This step measures; it changes no code that runs in the pipeline and moves no result. Its four
measurement questions — is the port deterministic at a size where rayon's work stealing really
interleaves, where does the CPU time go, how much memory does a scan cost, and do the per-pair
costs of D §1 hold — all have answers, and two of them contradict what D assumed:

* **D §5's shared `MatchData` LRU would remove 0.18 % of the CPU, not the pair-setup share.**
  R §1.2 sets `t_pair = min(t_A, t_B)`, so the expensive half of every pair is a rebuild at a
  thickness that belongs to exactly one partner and recurs nowhere in the collection. The cache
  can only ever drop the *cheap* half (§5).
* **The coarse score, 1.9 % of a pair in the Python profile, is 52 % of the port's largest
  development set.** Nothing about it got slower; stage 2 got 5–10× faster and the coarse score
  did not, and it is the one term that grows with the *product* of the two breaklines (§4.3).

Everything else holds. Determinism is byte-exact on both sets and at both thread counts (§2), the
per-pair cost is 3.0× and 5.2× inside D §1's two targets (§6), preprocessing is inside its 4 s
target (§6.1), and peak RSS is 2.0 GiB against D §8's 6 GB (§7).

**`tools/profile_split.py`** is the one file this step adds to the tree. `samply record
--save-only --unstable-presymbolicate` writes a profile whose frames are bare relative addresses
plus a sidecar with the demangled symbol table; the tool joins the two, expands every address of
the profiled binary through `atos -i` against a `dsymutil`-made `.dSYM` so that inlined frames
come back (at `codegen-units = 1` almost everything small is inlined), demangles through
`rustfilt`, and then reports self time per symbol and **inclusive** time per pipeline stage. Two
details of that are not obvious and both were found by getting the wrong answer first:

1. **Generic arguments have to go before the stage patterns are applied.** Rust's v0 mangling
   writes a method as `<Type>::method`, so a plain path never matches; and rayon's plumbing
   carries the closure it is driving inside its own type arguments, so
   `bridge_producer_consumer::helper::<…, match_pair::{closure#1}>` claims to be inside a stage
   it is only *about* to enter. `normalise` lifts the qualifier and drops the arguments; the
   arguments are then re-attached as a tail of "what this frame drives", which is the only
   evidence a stolen chunk carries about which par_iter it belongs to.
2. **A stolen job sits *below* its victim on the same stack.** Under nested parallelism a thread
   waiting inside one `join` steals work from anywhere in the pool, and the stolen frames are
   pushed onto the waiting stack. One stack out of the synthetic-20 profile, with the frame
   numbers left in so that the 140 frames of `rayon` and `core` plumbing between them are visible
   as the gaps they are:

   ```
   [ 56] sherd_core::matching::pair::match_pair          <- pair X
   [ 59] sherd_core::matching::coarse::scores            <- its coarse par_iter, waiting in join
   [ 79] rayon_core::registry::WorkerThread::wait_until
   [101] rayon_core::job::StackJob::execute              <- steals a block job
   [142] sherd_core::matching::pair::match_pair          <- pair Y
   [143] sherd_core::matching::pair::Pair::build
   [145] sherd_core::fragment::samples::MatchData::build
   ```

   Attributing that sample to `coarse` — which "outermost frame wins" does — charges one pair's
   array rebuild to another pair's coarse score. The tool cuts every stack at its **last** steal
   boundary and attributes below it. Before the cut, synthetic 20 read 46 % coarse / 17 % stage 2;
   after it, 52 % / 23 %. Unattributed CPU is **0.10 %** of the run.

Time is `threadCPUDelta` — microseconds of CPU actually burnt on that thread since the previous
sample — summed over every thread of the process, so every figure below is in **core-seconds** and
a share is a share of the machine's work, not of the wall clock.

### 4.2 Verify, in the port

R §6's five scores are 13.74 core-s on synthetic 20 and 12.74 on pot H. The self-time table says
where inside: `parry3d`'s point-to-triangle projection (R §6.1's fracture contact, 3.4 core-s on

### 4.3 Why the coarse share moves by 4× between two sets

The coarse score is `n_hyp × 60` bounded nearest-neighbour queries into A's breakline tree. Its
cost per hypothesis is a constant of the implementation and the measurement says so:

| set | hypotheses (C1 §4) | per pair | coarse | per hypothesis |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| synthetic 20 | 27 839 790 | 146 525 | 165.34 core-s | **5.94 µs** |
| pot H | 1 659 645 | 30 175 | 10.33 core-s | **6.22 µs** |

Six microseconds for sixty queries is 99 ns a query, and the two sets agree to 5 %. What differs
is the hypothesis count, and R §5.1 builds hypotheses from **pairs of breakline frames**, so it
grows with the product of the two subsampled breaklines — synthetic 20's fragments are 200 000-face
working meshes with long fracture edges, pot H's are 7 600–42 000 faces. Stage 2's cost, by
contrast, is set by cloud sizes that R §3.5 floors at 5 000 fracture and 6 000 margin points, so it
barely grows at all. **The coarse score is the term that scales with fragment size, and it is
therefore the term that decides the large collections.** An independent number from C1 §7 confirms
the total: `parity --stage coarse --injected` over synthetic 20's 190 pairs took 178 core-seconds
where this profile attributes 165.

## 5. What D §5's shared `MatchData` LRU would remove: 0.18 %

D §5 keeps a `Mutex<LruCache<(FragId, t_bits), Arc<MatchData>>>` of 64 entries "so a fragment
recomputed at `t_pair` for one pair serves the next". The premise does not hold, and it fails for a
reason that is in R §1.2 rather than in the port:

**`t_pair = min(t_A, t_B)`.** Of a pair's two builds, one is the fragment that *is* the minimum —
it is at its own `t`, `arrays_at` borrows its cached arrays and nothing is rebuilt — and the other
is the thicker fragment at a thickness that belongs to that one partner. Wall thicknesses are
`f64` measurements; two partners never produce the same one. So every expensive key is unique.

Counting the keys of the two sets in R §4.1's own pair order:

| set | pairs | `MatchData` builds | distinct keys | of which rebuild R §3.5 | rebuild keys that repeat |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| synthetic 20 | 190 | 380 | 209 | 190 | **0** |
| pot H | 55 | 110 | 65 | 55 | **0** |

And splitting `Pair::build`'s measured cost by whether `arrays_at` rebuilt:

| set | `Pair::build` | R §3.5 rebuilt at the partner's `t` | clouds + the two KD-trees (every build pays) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| synthetic 20 | 30.41 core-s (9.53 %) | 30.55 (9.58 %) | **≤ 0.63 (≤ 0.20 %)** |
| pot H | 1.43 core-s (1.75 %) | 1.37 (1.67 %) | **≤ 0.08 (≤ 0.09 %)** |

The LRU can only remove repeats, and the only repeats are the cheap half: 171 of synthetic 20's 190
own-`t` builds and 45 of pot H's 55. That is **at most 0.57 core-s of 319 (0.18 %)** and **at most
0.065 of 82.18 (0.08 %)**. D §5 has been amended to say so.

What *would* pay is making R §4.2's rebuild cheaper, because it is 9.6 % of the largest set and all
of it is recomputation. R §3.5 is not uniformly `t`-dependent: the breakline **points** are
midpoints of edges between a shell face and a fracture face and do not involve `t` at all, and
R §3.5.1's 20 000 whole-surface samples `S`/`sp` are drawn from face areas with a fixed seed and do
not either. What depends on `t` is the macro-normal ring (0.15 `t` – 0.60 `t`), the `0.5 t` voxel
subsample, `Pf`'s count `clip(⌊150·A_frac/t²⌋, 5000, 12000)` and the margin band
(0.12 `t` – 1.5 `t`). Splitting `breakline::build` into a `t`-free half computed once per fragment
and a `t`-dependent half computed per pair is the shape of that optimisation; it is a change to a
cached artefact and it has an exact gate already (`parity --stage breakline --stage samples`), so
it belongs in a step of its own, not in this one.

| `mixed_all` | 12 589 | 1.20 (pot H) | 15 056 | **39 min** | ≤ 2 h |

Both synthetic figures are upper bounds in the one way that matters: the 60- and 170-piece sets cut
the *same* pot the 20-piece set cuts, so their fragments are smaller than the ones the per-pair cost
was measured on — and §4.3 has just shown that the dominant term, the coarse score, grows with
fragment size. `mixed_all`'s 164 sherds are the same scans pot H's 11 come from, so its basis is a
like-for-like one. Preprocessing adds little: 170 scans of the synthetic kind at 3 s of core time
each is 8.5 core-minutes, under 2 minutes of wall clock at the concurrency §7 allows.

The one thing this projection cannot see is anything that is **not** linear in the pair count —
R §8's assembly over a few thousand accepted candidates, R §9's refinement over 170 placed
fragments, and the merged-assembly writer. On synthetic 20 those are 0.04 s and 2.14 s against
40.49 s of matching, so they would have to grow by two orders of magnitude to matter; that is the
kind of statement only the final acceptance run can settle.

## 7. Memory

### 7.1 One scan at a time

The seven single-scan runs of §6.1, fitted:

```
peak RSS  =  98.4 MiB  +  361 B per original face          (R² = 0.978, residuals ≤ 36 MiB)
          =  98 MiB    +  344 MiB per million faces
```

The fixed term is the *process*, not the scan — pay it once, however many scans run — so the
semaphore's per-job reservation is the **slope alone**. Three checks, the middle one being the
whole argument for that reading:

| concurrency | scans in flight | predicted | measured |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 1 scan, alone in its own process | `FY234094`, 1.34 M faces | 98 + 462 = **560 MiB** | 568 MiB |
| 4 (`segment --workers 4`, terracotta) | 4.33 M faces at once | 98 + 1 491 = **1 589 MiB** | 1 492 MiB (−6 %) |
| 9 (`run`, synthetic 20, cold) | the 9 largest of 8.10 M, 5.41 M | 98 + 1 861 = **1 959 MiB** | 2 038 MiB (+4 %) |

Charging the 98 MiB per *job* instead would predict 1 886 MiB for the four-concurrent row against
1 492 measured, so it is the process. The four-concurrent row is 6 % *under* even the one-floor
prediction, because four scans do not reach their own peaks at the same instant — which makes a
reservation on this model slightly conservative, the right direction for a semaphore.

One observation the model does not cover: `segment --workers 1` over the same four scans
**sequentially** peaks at 786 MiB, 218 MiB above the largest scan alone. Nothing is concurrent
there; it is macOS's allocator holding freed pages between one scan and the next. It does not
change the reservation — which has to be sized on what runs *at the same time* — but it is why a
run's peak RSS is always somewhat above the sum the semaphore admitted.

D §5 guessed `60 MB + 110 B·f`. The fixed part is 1.7× low and the per-face part **3.3×** low, and
D §5 has been corrected (it said "re-measure in E1").

### 7.2 The whole run

| run | shape | peak RSS | D §8's gate |
|---|---|---:|---|
| terracotta `segment`, `--workers 1` | cold, 4 scans sequential | 786 MiB | — |
| terracotta `segment`, `--workers 4` | cold, 4 concurrent | 1 492 MiB | — |
| terracotta `segment`, `--workers 10` | cold, 4 concurrent (only 4 exist) | 1 546 MiB | — |
| pot H `run`, 9 threads, no previews/meshes | warm | 280 MiB | — |
| pot H `run`, default | cold, previews + meshes | 479 MiB | — |
| synthetic 20 `run`, no previews/meshes | warm | 1 280 MiB | — |
| synthetic 20 `run`, default | cold, previews + meshes | **2 038 MiB** | ≤ 6 GB |

The largest development set peaks at **2.0 GiB against 6 GB**, and §7.1 says where: the peak is the
preprocessing stage with nine scans in flight, not the matching stage (which the warm run measures
at 1 280 MiB) and not the writers.

### 7.3 What the semaphore has to hold 170 scans to — a projection

Two budgets are worth quoting, because they give different answers.

| scan size | reservation | at D §5's default budget (50 % of 16 GB = 8 GiB) | at 4.5 GiB, which is what keeps the *whole run* under D §8's 6 GB |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 M faces | 344 MiB | 23 concurrent | 13 concurrent |
| **2 M faces** | 688 MiB | 11 concurrent | **6 concurrent** |
| 4 M faces | 1 376 MiB | 5 | 3 |
| 10 M faces | 3 440 MiB | 2 | 1 |

So: **for 170 scans of 1–2 M faces on this 16 GB machine the semaphore has to hold the pool to 6
concurrent jobs**, and at 1 M faces it never binds below the default pool of 9. The 4.5 GiB column
is the one that matters, and it is 6 GB minus what the rest of the run needs at that size: 170
fragments' working meshes, labels, breaklines and match arrays stay resident for the whole matching
stage, and synthetic 20 measures that at 1 280 MiB for 20 fragments of 200 000 faces — 64 MiB per
fragment, or 335 B per working-mesh face. A 170-piece cut of the same pot has ~8.5× less area per
fragment, so ~1.3 GiB resident; `mixed_all`'s 164 sherds at pot H's ~23 000 working faces average
come to ~1.2 GiB. Either way ≈ 1.3 GiB of the 6 GB is spoken for before a scan is opened, and the
preprocessing budget is what is left.

D §8's own row said "3 concurrent 10 M-face scans"; the measured constant says **2** at the 8 GiB
budget and 1 at 4.5 GiB. D §8 has been corrected.

## 8. Thread scaling on 6 P-cores + 4 E-cores

pot H, warm cache, `--no-preview --no-meshes`, the `matching` stage as `report.json` times it.
Every one of the seven runs produced the same joins and the same report outside `timings`.

| threads | matching | speed-up | CPU | parallel efficiency | peak RSS |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 65.26 s | 1.00× | 64.79 core-s | 100 % | 134 MiB |
| 2 | 33.05 s | 1.97× | 65.69 | 98.7 % | 152 MiB |
| 4 | 16.95 s | 3.85× | 66.95 | 96.3 % | 179 MiB |
| 6 | 11.72 s | 5.57× | 68.15 | 92.8 % | 229 MiB |
| 8 | 10.84 s | 6.02× | 76.56 | 75.3 % | 271 MiB |
| **9 (default)** | **10.12 s** | **6.45×** | 79.63 | 71.7 % | 280 MiB |
| 10 | 10.05 s | 6.49× | 78.54 | 64.9 % | 304 MiB |

The shape is the machine's, not the port's. Up to six threads the pool is on performance cores and
scaling is 93 % efficient with the CPU cost flat at 65–68 core-s. The seventh thread onwards lands
on efficiency cores: wall clock still falls, but only from 11.72 s to 10.05 s (**14 %**), while the
same work now costs 78.5 core-s instead of 68.2 (**+15 %**) — the E-cores retire the same
instructions more slowly, so the identical work bills more core-seconds. Note also that the
single-thread total, 65.79 core-s of per-pair time (§6), is 17 % below the 79.6 core-s the
nine-thread run burns: that gap *is* the E-cores plus the stealing.

**The port should keep its default of `cores − 1` (9 here).** Three reasons, all in the table.
It is within 0.7 % of the best wall clock the machine can produce, so nothing is left on it. Sizing
the pool at 6 to stay on performance cores would cost 16 % of wall clock, so the E-cores are worth
taking. And leaving one core unclaimed keeps the machine usable during a run and costs nothing
measurable, which is why `cli.py` chose it (V4-D8) and why the port copied it. No affinity work is
needed: rayon's work stealing already handles the asymmetric cores — a thread on an E-core simply
finishes fewer candidates, and since every parallel section collects by index, which thread did
what changes no answer (§2 measures exactly that).

## 9. The writers

| set | R §11.4 meshes | R §11.5 previews | R §11.1–11.3 text | total CPU | share of CPU | `output` stage wall | share of wall | cores busy while writing |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| synthetic 20 | 2.64 core-s | 2.30 | < 0.06 | **4.94 core-s** | **1.55 %** | 5.58 s of 48.31 | **11.6 %** | 0.89 |
| pot H | 0.33 | 1.18 | < 0.02 | **1.51 core-s** | **1.8 %** | 1.55 s of 12.32 | **12.6 %** | 0.97 |

The two numbers that matter are the two that disagree. The writers are **1.6 % of the machine's
work and 12 % of the user's wait**, because they run at ~0.9 cores: R §11.4 streams one placed mesh
at a time by design (D §5 keeps it that way so no merged mesh is ever in memory) and R §11.5's
renderer walks the groups in order. Turning them off confirms it — the same synthetic-20 run with
`--no-preview --no-meshes` reports `output 0.00 s` and finishes in 45.84 s instead of 48.31 s.

A bounded parallel writer — two or three placed meshes in flight instead of one — would recover
most of that 12 % at a memory cost the semaphore of §7 already knows how to price, and it cannot
change a byte of any file: the meshes are independent and their names are fixed. It is the cheapest
remaining wall-clock win outside the matcher.

## 10. What phase 1e should do next, in the order the measurements rank it

1. **The coarse score (52 % of synthetic 20, 6 µs per hypothesis).** Every one of its 60 queries
   per hypothesis is `PointTree::nearest_below(&p, delta)` — the *nearest* breakline point within a
   radius, whose normal is then tested. That is a bounded radius query, not an unbounded
   nearest-neighbour search, and D §6.2 already specifies a uniform hash grid for exactly this. A
   grid at cell = `sc.coarse` answers it by scanning 27 cells; it must return the same neighbour
   `kiddo` returns, ties included, and there is an exact gate for that — the `coarse` parity row
   compares `cs` bit for bit on 38.1 M hypotheses across eight sets (C1 §4). This is also the term
   that decides the large collections (§4.3) and the one D §6.5 already plans as a GPU kernel.
2. **R §4.2's rebuild (9.6 % of synthetic 20, all of it recomputation).** Split R §3.5 into its
   `t`-free half (breakline points, the whole-surface samples) and its `t`-dependent half; §5 says
   why the LRU is not the answer and what is.
3. **The writers (12 % of wall, 1.6 % of CPU).** Two or three placed meshes in flight (§9).
4. **Do not build D §5's `MatchData` LRU** until (2) has changed the arithmetic: as the code stands
   it is worth 0.18 % (§5).
5. **Leave the pool at `cores − 1`** (§8), and leave the ICP alone — it is already 5–10× the
   reference (C2 §7) and it is no longer the dominant term on the set that decides the gates.

Nothing in that list changes a threshold or an answer, and each item has an exact parity row or a
byte-comparison behind it. The gate for every one of them is the one this note establishes in §2:
the same outputs, byte for byte.
